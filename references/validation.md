# Risk validation and PMF interviews

Source: “Risk Validation Techniques” through the validation module recap, original lines 1007–3005.

## Pick the risk before the technique

Start with broad validation: market research, expert advice, and PMF interviews. Reuse existing credible evidence rather than repeating completed research.

For research, seek **analogs** (successful aspects of comparable products) and **antilogs** (failed aspects). A single company can provide both across different dimensions. Explain why each comparison transfers to this segment and business. Relevant builders or domain experts can challenge those comparisons; their opinions remain evidence to evaluate, not proof.

Produce a compact risk register:

| Dimension and specific hypothesis | Supporting evidence | Contradicting evidence / unknowns | Conviction and reason | Domino effect | Next test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Prioritize low-conviction hypotheses. Where several compete, test the one whose failure would invalidate the most consequential connected dimensions: the **domino effect**. Distinguish a risky dimension (“business model”) from a testable hypothesis (“this buyer will pay this price for this outcome”). Avoid numerical scoring that conceals weak evidence.

## Prepare interviews

Recruit across hypothesized Now and relevant Future segments so the target audience itself can be tested. The course suggests an initial 3–5 interviews per segment and roughly 30–50 over several rounds; adapt to diversity, saturation, resources, and the decision. These counts do not establish statistical representativeness.

Plan → source → contact → screen. Screen for relevant attributes and actual experience; account for user/buyer roles. Specify logistics, consent, note capture, and proportionate compensation. Draft outreach when useful; sending it requires the user's authorization.

## Conduct interviews

The course suggests 60–90 minutes: rapport (~5), build (~10), peak (~40–70), and wrap-up (~5). Adjust to scope while preserving coverage of consequential assumptions.

Use **unaided questions → participant ranking → aided questions → combined ranking**. Capture spontaneous responses before exposing the hypothesis. Ask for recent concrete experiences and tradeoffs, then explore the proposed benefits. Do not interpret politeness or hypothetical purchase intent as observed behavior.

Adapt these prompts:

| Dimension | Unaided exploration | Aided follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Tell me about your role, situation, and how this work happens. | Clarify missing attributes; explore newly surfaced ones. |
| Problem | Walk me through the last time you tried to achieve the outcome. What was hardest? | Introduce hypothesized gaps, ask whether they occurred, and rerank severity. |
| Value | What would a substantially better outcome look like? | Present benefit statements; rank them and ask why each matters or fails to matter. |
| Competition | How do you solve this now? What have you tried, paid for, or abandoned? | Compare relevant alternatives and explore concrete switching barriers. |
| Growth | How did you discover and evaluate your current solution? Whose advice did you use? | Explore plausible channels, invitations, or content without assuming future sharing. |
| Business model | What does the problem cost today? Who owns the budget and decision? | Discuss proposed pricing, buying process, objections, and what commitment is possible. |

Close by asking what was missed and clarifying any follow-up permission.

## Debrief and synthesize

Debrief promptly. Separate direct statements/observations from interpretation, and aided from unaided responses. Capture first-order takeaways by dimension and second-order implications: new insights, repeated patterns, conflicting cases, and changes needed in subsequent interviews.

Compare patterns within and across segments. Include counts and denominators when available, without extrapolating convenience samples to the market. Distinguish respondents, companies, and buying units. A repeated pattern is a lead to investigate, not a vote that establishes PMF. Where multiple researchers attended, compare interpretations against the recorded evidence.

Stop or change technique when themes converge with adequate segment coverage; investigate inconsistent responses before declaring saturation. Update the narrative, then reprioritize risks.

## Targeted technique menu

Choose by hypothesis fit first, then cost, time, and access. These are the source's primary use cases, not an assertion that other learning is impossible.

| Technique | Primary question it can address | Limits and practical fit |
|---|---|---|
| Survey | How widely do problem, segment, benefit, and pricing hypotheses resonate? | Useful after interviews, especially with an existing audience; stated preferences need behavioral follow-up. |
| Smoke test | Will a specified segment respond to the problem/benefit promise? | Track qualified visits and explicit conversions; clicks or waitlists do not establish payment, delivery value, or retention. |
| Prototype | Does the proposed experience communicate and deliver the intended benefit relative to alternatives? | Useful for value and differentiation; task feedback is not repeat usage or willingness to pay. |
| Pre-sale | Will the buyer commit at the proposed price, and what blocks the purchase? | Stronger willingness-to-pay evidence; distinguish verbal interest, nonbinding intent, refundable deposit, and paid commitment. State delivery terms honestly. |
| Product test | Does real use deliver value, beat alternatives, and support monetization? | Include an actual payment decision if pricing is the hypothesis. Use the smallest credible concierge, no-code, manual-backed, or coded experience. |
| Growth test | Can the intended channel acquire the right users at viable cost? | Measure downstream activation/quality as well as acquisition. Timing depends on the channel; a short test cannot settle a slow content loop. |

Consider cheaper techniques before building, but use a product test directly when the hypothesis depends on an experience people cannot evaluate abstractly, such as a social product. A free MVP cannot validate willingness to pay.

## Experiment brief

For the selected test specify:

- Hypothesis, segment, and decision it informs.
- Method, recruitment source, exposure, and observable commitment or behavior.
- Primary metric with numerator, denominator, observation window, and exclusions.
- Proposed success, failure, and inconclusive criteria with rationale; distinguish these from established benchmarks.
- Time, budget, sample/access constraints, and confounds.
- What each result changes in the narrative and what happens next.

Keep building, publishing, spending, contacting participants, and collecting payments within the user's actual authorization. A strategy request produces a concrete test plan, not automatic execution of external experiments.

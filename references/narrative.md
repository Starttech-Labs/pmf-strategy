# Building and reviewing a PMF narrative

Source: “Creating Your Initial PMF Narrative” through “Business Model and PMF Narrative Synthesis,” original lines 142–954.

## Initial insight

Pressure-test whether the insight is earned from experience, observed behavior, or immersion; differentiated from obvious consensus; and grounded in at least one strategy dimension. Disagreement can expose a distinctive insight, but disagreement alone is not evidence it is correct. For expansion within an existing business, identify the existing assets or strategy dimensions that make this organization especially equipped to win.

## Six dimensions

### 1. Problem to solve

Use **outcome → motivation → gap**. What is the customer trying to achieve? Why does it matter? What prevents it today? Describe the unmet gap as a customer problem, with urgency, frequency, consequences, and current workarounds where known. Avoid retrofitting a problem to a preferred feature. “Scheduling takes hours of back-and-forth” describes a problem; “customers need our scheduling assistant” embeds the solution.

### 2. Target audience

Identify relevant demographic, psychographic, or position/context attributes, then combine them into plausible segments. Two or three meaningful attributes are a useful starting point, not a quota. For B2B, distinguish the end user, buyer, and decision maker; for marketplaces, identify each side.

Sequence segments into **Now** and **Future**. Choose the initial niche by the combined severity of its problem, benefit resonance, competitive gap, reachability, and willingness to pay. Explain the bridge to adjacent segments. A small existing niche can be a good starting point; a broad market-size claim is not a targeting strategy. Keep the segment choice provisional until validated.

### 3. Value proposition

Use the **ideal homepage** exercise: one clear value promise and roughly three to five distinct sub-benefits. Express customer outcomes before features. Each benefit should address a stated gap for the chosen audience. A longer first draft can be narrowed through validation. Do not turn this exercise into building a website unless asked.

### 4. Competitive advantage

Explain **why this segment chooses us today** and **what could sustain that advantage later**.

Map direct competitors, indirect alternatives (including manual work and doing nothing), and adjacent products that could become competitors. Identify whom each alternative serves well and the underserved segment where this product can win.

For durability, the source uses Hamilton Helmer's seven powers: scale economies, network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resource, and process power. Its emphasis is on the first four:

- Scale economies: per-unit cost declines with volume.
- Network economies: additional participants increase customer value.
- Counter-positioning: incumbents face damage to their existing business if they copy the model.
- Switching costs: customers lose meaningful value when changing suppliers.

Name the mechanism and prerequisites, not just a moat label. Separate an existing advantage from a hypothesis about a future one. Novel features and being first do not by themselves establish durable protection.

### 5. Growth strategy

Separate short-term traction that supplies research participants and early users from a repeatable growth loop. Explain how the initial niche can be reached, how later cohorts help acquire subsequent cohorts, and why the value proposition and economics support the loop. Read [Growth](growth.md) when selecting or designing channels.

### 6. Business model

Describe the business equation as interacting levers, not merely a revenue source or a forced algebraic formula: revenue streams, pricing/payment model and willingness to pay, customer lifetime value, and major fixed and variable costs. Include acquisition costs and the path to viable margins where relevant.

Possible levers by model:

| Model | Levers to examine |
|---|---|
| Freemium SaaS | Signups, activation/active use, free-to-paid conversion, selling price, churn, CAC, payback |
| Enterprise SaaS | Opportunities, win rate, contract value, net revenue retention, sales cost |
| Marketplace | Transaction volume, buyer/seller liquidity, repeat usage, take rate, service and acquisition costs |
| Commerce | Traffic, purchase conversion, order value, purchase frequency, margin, fulfillment and acquisition costs |

Label unknown values. State whether lifetime value means revenue or contribution profit; do not mix them when comparing costs. Use ranges only when supported or explicitly proposed as hypotheses.

## Synthesis and critique

Write the narrative in connected prose, with headings for the six dimensions if helpful. A checklist or canvas alone does not demonstrate coherence. Adapt length to the audience; use a separate evidence/risk table so the prose remains readable.

Check the connections:

- Is the urgent problem actually felt by the Now audience?
- Do the benefits resolve it better than the alternatives that audience uses?
- Is the proposed price justified to the buyer as well as useful to the end user?
- Can the channel reach that audience at a cost the business can support?
- Does the product enable the proposed loop, and can the strategy develop its claimed moat?
- Does expansion preserve these connections, or require fresh validation?

For a review, give the strongest and weakest dimensions, cross-dimension contradictions, and concrete revisions. For a new draft, end with the riskiest hypothesis and missing evidence. Classify updates as a refinement (sharper existing hypothesis), pivot (materially different dimension), or reset (the foundation no longer holds). Propagate a change across affected dimensions.
